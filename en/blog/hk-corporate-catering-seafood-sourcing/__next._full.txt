1b:Td4b,Corporate events use different food service formats than weddings. The format dictates portion size, prep timing, and order math.

**Format 1: Seated plated dinner (5- to 7-course)**
Most similar to wedding banquet sourcing math. Premium seafood appears in:
• Course 1 (amuse-bouche or canapé course): salmon tartare spoon (15–25g per guest) or uni-on-toast (5–10g per guest)
• Course 2 (cold appetiser): salmon sashimi rosettes (30–50g per guest) or seafood medley with uni accent (10–15g uni per guest)
• Occasionally a dedicated uni course: 8–15g uni per guest as a feature plate

Volume math for 300-guest gala dinner with salmon + uni amuse:
• Salmon tartare: 20g × 300 × 1.1 buffer = 6.6 kg
• Uni accent: 8g × 300 × 1.15 buffer = 2.8 kg

**Format 2: Canapé + cocktail reception (no seated meal)**
Dominant for product launches and networking-focused events. Different math:
• Canapés are sized at 4–10g of premium ingredient per piece
• Standard pacing: 6–10 canapés per guest across 90–120 minutes
• Of which 30–40% are seafood (3–4 pieces per guest)
• Of which premium uni canapés are typically 0.5–1 piece per guest, salmon canapés 1.5–2.5 pieces per guest

Volume math for 200-guest product launch cocktail (90 min):
• Salmon canapés: 2 pieces × 200 × 8g = 3.2 kg + 15% canapé waste/breakage = 3.7 kg
• Uni canapés: 1 piece × 200 × 6g = 1.2 kg + 15% buffer = 1.4 kg

**Format 3: Buffet station + carving station**
Common for large conferences (HKCEC events). Different again:
• Buffet portions size larger (60–100g per pass on average)
• But not every guest visits every station; sashimi stations typically attract 40–60% of guests at a given pass
• Multiple service passes: lunch buffet often pulses (12:00 / 13:00 / 13:30)
• Replenishment timing is critical — fresh sashimi can sit on ice no longer than 30 minutes on station

Volume math for 600-guest conference buffet with sashimi station (90 min lunch):
• Salmon sashimi: 60g × 600 × 50% (visit rate) × 1.2 (replenishment buffer) = 21.6 kg
• Multiple replenishment cycles: 4 × 5.4 kg trays, prepped and held in chiller

**Format 4: VIP/executive side rooms (within larger event)**
A hidden ops complexity: many corporate events have a separate VIP room with elevated menu (uni feature, A5 wagyu, etc.). Volumes are small (10–40 guests) but cost-per-guest is 2–4x main floor and lead time is the same.
• Always sized separately — never roll into main floor allocation
• Confirm 4 weeks ahead and lock supplier allocation specifically for this micro-volume

**Plate-up labour and timing implications:**

• Plated dinner: kitchen needs salmon delivered Friday morning, prepped Friday afternoon, cut Saturday morning
• Canapé event: needs prep day-of (Friday for Friday-evening events) for maximum freshness; longer-storage canapés can prep day-before
• Buffet: needs prep morning-of with held replenishment trays
• VIP room: prep same as main format but with extra portion control

1c:Td6d,Corporate event procurement runs on different rhythms than wedding banquets. Understanding the actual lead time available is the difference between locked allocation and last-minute scrambling.

**Typical corporate event procurement timeline:**

• **8–16 weeks ahead**: Event agency or in-house planner sends out venue RFP, gets initial budgets approved
• **6–10 weeks ahead**: Venue + caterer selected; menu shortlist circulated for tasting
• **4–6 weeks ahead**: Final menu selected; preliminary headcount given (subject to ±20% movement)
• **2–4 weeks ahead**: Headcount narrows to ±10%; F&B procurement should lock supplier allocation
• **1 week ahead**: Final headcount with +5/-5% expected variance
• **48 hours ahead**: Final headcount locked, kitchen prep begins
• **Event day**: Service

**The 2–4 week allocation window is the critical procurement moment.** This is when:
• Headcount is reliable enough to commit volume
• Supplier can still confirm allocation for the event week
• Premium grades (uni AA Bafun) are still available rather than spot-bought

**The forecasting calculation corporate catering teams should run weekly:**

```
For each confirmed event in the next 6 weeks:
  Format-adjusted volume × headcount = base requirement
  + format-appropriate buffer (10–30%)
  = order quantity

Aggregate by week:
  Week of [date]: total uni + total salmon required across all events
  Compare to supplier confirmed allocation
  Flag gaps to procurement at 4-week mark, not 1-week mark
```

**The headcount-swing problem specific to corporate events:**

Unlike weddings where ±2 tables is the upper limit, corporate events frequently see ±20% headcount swings 2–3 weeks before event date, driven by:

• Executive registration deadlines that slip
• Final RSVP push to junior staff in the last 2 weeks
• Late VIP additions (board, investors, key clients)
• Travel disruptions (typhoon season, regional flights)

Procurement protocols that handle this well:

• **Tier allocation**: Lock 90% of expected volume firm at 4 weeks; hold 10–15% headroom with supplier for late additions
• **Substitution-up policy**: If final headcount exceeds order, supplier commits to upgrade alternative (e.g., A-grade Murasaki instead of B-grade if more volume required at last minute)
• **Stable downside**: Define minimum guaranteed pay even if headcount comes in lower than ordered — protects supplier and incentivizes them to hold capacity for you

**Last-minute event lead-time scenarios:**

Not all corporate events have 6+ weeks of notice. Common short-lead scenarios:

• **Crisis comms or PR-driven event** (e.g., reputational response, M&A announcement): 1–2 weeks lead, modest volume
• **Executive visit / dignitary** (e.g., regional president visit): 2–4 weeks, small but high-stakes
• **Re-scheduled events** (e.g., weather displacement): same volume, half the lead time
• **Last-minute addition to existing series** (e.g., extra investor lunch added during AGM week): 1–3 weeks

• Verify supplier inventory immediately — written confirmation within 24 hours
• Accept grade flexibility if needed (A vs. AA) — disclose substitution to event lead
• Use established supplier relationship rather than spot-buying — protects margin

1d:Tf60,The defining operational challenge of corporate catering: many venues are not chef-controlled kitchens. Conference centres, art galleries, yachts, outdoor venues, retail spaces, and private estates have variable cold-storage capability and unpredictable temperature control.

**Venue-type cold-chain risk profile:**

• **5-star hotel ballrooms**: Full kitchen support, walk-in chillers, controlled cold-chain. **Low risk.**
• **HKCEC + AsiaWorld-Expo**: On-site kitchens for caterers + dedicated cold storage. **Low-medium risk.** Confirm specific bay assignment 1 week ahead.
• **Hotel meeting rooms with off-property F&B**: Cooler trucks + small holding chillers. **Medium risk.** Need backup capacity plan.
• **Art galleries + retail spaces**: Limited or no on-site cold storage. **High risk.** Must use insulated transport with continuous temperature logging.
• **Yacht charters**: Galley refrigeration is small, often shared with operating provisions. **High risk.** Need pre-event prep approach with minimal on-vessel holding.
• **Outdoor venues + private estates**: No infrastructure assumed. **Highest risk.** Refrigerated truck stays on-site as cold-chain anchor.

**The cold-chain protocol for non-kitchen venues:**

**Step 1 — Pre-event venue assessment (2 weeks before)**
• Walk venue with caterer + supplier
• Identify cold storage available, capacity, temperature settings
• Map refrigerated truck access and parking
• Identify ice supply (ice sculpture station can double as ice supply for stations)
• Document gaps in writing

**Step 2 — Day-of cold-chain plan**
• Salmon and uni arrive at venue ≤ 2 hours before service start
• If venue has no cold storage: refrigerated truck stays on-site as cold storage; product transferred to stations in 20-min batches
• Temperature loggers attached to product crates during transport (≤4°C target for salmon, ≤2°C target for uni)
• Pre-event temperature check documented; rejection threshold defined in writing

**Step 3 — Service-period replenishment**
• Uni canapé stations: replenish every 20 minutes max; never let trays sit longer
• Salmon sashimi stations: replenish every 30 minutes; rotate platters from chiller-held backup
• Buffet salmon stations: longer hold acceptable (up to 90 min with proper ice) but quality degrades
• Floating ice underneath display platters refreshed every 30 min

**Step 4 — Post-event documentation**
• Temperature log retained for 30 days minimum
• Any food safety incident escalated within 24 hours
• Yield-vs-order documented for forecast refinement

**The expensive lesson learned by HK event caterers:** An outdoor product launch in late September with a sashimi station — no refrigerated truck, expectation that 'a few coolers would be enough.' Within 90 minutes the salmon was hitting 8–10°C. The brand impression was damaged when guests visibly hesitated at the station; one media attendee photographed the temperature display. The caterer lost the renewal contract worth HKD $2.4M over 24 months. The marginal cost of the refrigerated truck would have been HKD $4,500.

**Backup contingency for high-stakes events:**

• Secondary supplier confirmed available with same-day emergency delivery to venue location
• On-venue backup chiller (rental cost ~HKD $1,500/day for portable unit) for non-kitchen sites
• Pre-arranged escalation chain: who decides to invoke contingency, in what time window
• For yacht events: shadow refrigerated cooler at marina pier as backup

**Refrigerated transport criteria specific to corporate venues:**

When evaluating supplier delivery capability, ask:
• Is the delivery vehicle GPS-tracked with temperature telemetry?
• Can you provide a temperature log printout on delivery?
• What is your max time-window for last-mile delivery once the truck is loaded?

**Where margin leaks in corporate catering seafood operations:**

• **Headcount over-ordering**: ±20% headcount swing means caterers often over-order by 15–20% as a 'safety' buffer. Over a year, this compounds to 5–8% of seafood spend in waste. Use tier-allocation discipline instead.
• **Last-minute spot purchases**: When supplier allocation falls short, spot purchases can cost 25–40% above committed prices. Direct cause: lead-time discipline failures.
• **Format-mismatch sizing**: Sizing a canapé event with plated-dinner volumes (40g salmon per guest) wastes 50% of product. Build format-specific calculation templates.
• **Cold-chain failure write-offs**: Non-kitchen-venue cold-chain failures destroy product within 2 hours. Backup cold-storage rental at HKD $1,500/day pays for itself with the first prevented incident.
• **Unbilled venue + transport surcharges**: Many caterers absorb refrigerated transport and venue cold-chain rental rather than pricing them into client quotes. Itemize these in proposals.
• **VIP-room undersized orders**: Forgetting to size separately for VIP rooms is the second most common margin leak (after format-mismatch sizing).
