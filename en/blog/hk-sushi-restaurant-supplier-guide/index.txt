**Grade hierarchy for sushi use:**

• **AA Bafun (Hokkaido)**: smallest, sweetest, most intense flavor; lobes are firm and hold shape on rice; standard for premium omakase. Wholesale HKD $850–$1,400 per 100g uni board (板). Service yield: 50–60g of plate-ready uni per board.

• **AA Murasaki (Hokkaido)**: larger lobes, milder flavor, longer service window before texture degrades; very common at HKD $700–$1,500 nigiri-counters. Wholesale HKD $580–$950 per 100g board. Service yield: 60–75g per board.

• **A Murasaki**: still board (板) presentation, slightly less uniform lobe size, occasional broken pieces; the workhorse of mid-tier sushi-ya. Wholesale HKD $420–$680 per 100g board. Service yield: 55–70g per board.

• **B-grade or non-board pack (gunkan grade)**: pre-broken lobes, often sold by the gram in a tub rather than as a 100g board. Suitable for gunkan (battleship) maki where presentation tolerates imperfect lobes. Wholesale HKD $250–$420 per 100g. Service yield: ~90% (less trim waste).

• **Maine (US East Coast)**: larger, paler, less sweet; lower cost and longer cold-chain reliability (different sea route). Common in kaiten and as a B-tier substitute. Wholesale HKD $180–$320 per 100g.

**Board (板) vs tray (托) presentation:**

Premium omakase requires uni delivered on the original wooden Hokkaido board (板) intact, with lobes visible and arranged. Mid-tier and kaiten can use tray-packed (托) presentations where lobes are placed individually after thaw or after pre-portioning. Confirm with your supplier whether the board comes intact and what the substitution policy is if a board arrives partially broken — this is the single most common quality dispute in HK sushi procurement.

**Seasonal availability and supplier sourcing:**

• **Hokkaido Bafun**: peak season June–September; reduced availability October–May (Maine substitution common)
• **Hokkaido Murasaki**: more consistent year-round; small price spike November–February
• **Maine**: year-round, with summer (June–August) tighter due to US domestic demand
• **Russian Sakhalin / Chilean**: occasionally on HK market — disclose to omakase guests, never substitute silently for advertised Hokkaido

**Origin profiles for sushi service:**

• **Norwegian (Norway sea-farmed Atlantic salmon)**: highest fat content (typically 14–18% intramuscular), large fillet size, even fat distribution, mild flavor. Standard choice for HK sushi-ya across price tiers. Wholesale HKD $130–$180 per kg (full sides), HKD $160–$240 per kg (portioned sashimi blocks). Sushi-grade certification standard with most suppliers.

• **Faroe Islands**: similar to Norwegian but slightly firmer texture and 12–16% fat. Increasingly preferred by sushi chefs who want texture (firmer bite) without losing fat-driven flavor. Wholesale HKD $145–$200 per kg. Less consistent supply chain than Norway.

• **Tasmanian (Australian)**: very high fat content (16–20%), darker color, fuller flavor; some chefs prefer Tasmanian belly for fatty (toro) presentations. Wholesale HKD $170–$240 per kg. Shorter air-freight route to HK = slightly fresher arrival, but supply is more variable.

• **Scottish**: similar fat profile to Norwegian (12–16%), slightly different flavor; ASC and RSPCA certifications often easier to obtain. Wholesale HKD $135–$190 per kg.

• **Chilean (Atlantic salmon farmed in Chile)**: lower wholesale cost, but historically variable on sea-lice antibiotic regulations and freshness due to long air-freight route. Standard for kaiten and budget operations. Wholesale HKD $90–$140 per kg. Inspect each shipment carefully.

• **Wild salmon (Pacific species: King, Coho, Sockeye)**: rarely used for sushi nigiri in HK due to lower fat content and tighter texture (better cooked); occasionally featured as a seasonal omakase piece. Not a routine procurement category for sushi-ya.

**Cut specification matters as much as origin:**

Most suppliers offer salmon in three cut formats. Your menu requirements drive which mix you need:

• **Full sides (3–5 kg per side)**: best yield economics, but requires in-house breakdown skill; standard for omakase and full-service sushi-ya
• **Pre-portioned blocks (saku, 200–500g)**: 15–25% cost premium, but eliminates breakdown labor and gives consistent portion control; standard for kaiten and high-volume operations
• **Sliced sashimi (pre-cut nigiri-ready)**: 40–60% cost premium, used by smaller hotel counters and convenience-format operations; ages faster after cut so must be used within 24 hours of delivery

**The cold-chain-specific concern for sushi salmon:**

Salmon for sushi service must arrive at ≤4°C with no temperature deviation during transport. The cold-chain failure indicator most often missed by sushi-ya is the day-3-after-delivery test: salmon that was thermally abused during transport will start showing brown discoloration along the bloodline and lose firmness on the cutting board within 48–72 hours of arrival — by which time the supplier will deny responsibility. 1b:Te21,Sushi service has cold-chain requirements that differ from restaurant operations broadly. The fish is consumed raw, sliced fresh-to-order or prepped within a 4–6 hour service window, and judged on micron-level visual quality at the counter. Cold-chain discipline is the difference between menu spec and reality.

**Delivery window timing for sushi-ya:**

• **Optimal**: delivery 2–4 hours before opening service, allowing temperature stabilization and pre-service prep
• **Acceptable**: delivery night before with overnight controlled chiller storage (≤2°C for uni, ≤4°C for salmon)
• **Suboptimal**: delivery during service hours (disrupts prep flow + inspection rushed)
• **Unacceptable**: delivery at temperatures above protocol — reject and document

**Inspection protocol on arrival (every shipment, no exceptions):**

• **Uni boards**: check seal intact, board surface uniformly cold to touch (≤2°C), lobes intact and golden-colored (not dull or melted), no liquid pooling on board
• **Salmon sides**: check core temperature with probe thermometer (≤4°C), color uniformity along bloodline (no brown patches), fat distribution visible and even, no sour/ammonia odor
• **Pre-portioned salmon**: check vacuum-pack integrity, color, no liquid in pack

Document every inspection with a written log (digital or paper). Suppliers who take quality seriously will support this; suppliers who push back are the ones who will dispute quality issues later.

**Service-shift product handling:**

• **Pre-service prep**: uni boards transferred to working chiller 30 minutes before service start; salmon broken down to portioned blocks 60–90 minutes before service
• **During service**: uni held at ≤2°C in counter chiller, never on open counter; salmon held at ≤4°C, brought up to slicing in 200–400g portions
• **Between services**: all product returned to walk-in chiller, never left at room temperature even briefly
• **End of service**: yield documentation — actual portions served vs prep portions, with carryover to next service tracked

**The 18-hour clock for uni:**

Uni boards have a sharp quality cliff after 18 hours from the harvest-pack to the counter — texture starts to soften, lobes lose definition, and the visual signal of 'fresh uni' begins to degrade. The procurement implication: order frequency for premium omakase should ideally be daily; mid-tier sushi-ya can run on 3-times-per-week if cold-chain is disciplined; kaiten can run on 2-times-per-week with quality compromise accepted.

**The 72-hour window for salmon nigiri:**

Salmon retains nigiri-quality texture and color for approximately 72 hours after delivery at ≤4°C. Beyond 72 hours, color fades and texture softens. Procurement frequency for sushi-ya is typically twice-weekly (Monday + Thursday delivery, for example) to keep all served product within the 72-hour window.

**Day-of-delivery service flow that protects quality:**

A disciplined HK sushi-ya delivery day looks like this:

• 09:00 — supplier delivers, head chef inspects, signs off or rejects
• 09:30 — temperature documented; product transferred to walk-in chiller in original packaging
• 11:00 — pre-service prep begins; uni boards moved to counter chiller; salmon sides broken down
• 11:30 — lunch service
• 14:30 — between-service reset; carryover yield documented
• 17:30 — dinner service prep
• 23:00 — end-of-day yield log; carryover items flagged for next-day priority

1c:T11c4,Generic supplier criteria apply (transparency, traceability, certifications). Sushi operations layer on four sushi-specific evaluation criteria.

**1. Grade-and-origin honesty**

The single most common quality issue in HK sushi procurement is silent grade or origin substitution. The discipline:

• Demand written grade and origin documentation on every shipment
• Reject substitution without prior notice — even when the substitution is technically equivalent or upward
• Track substitution frequency by supplier over a quarter; suppliers above 5% silent-substitution rate fail evaluation
• For omakase operations: require lot-level traceability that you can reference if a guest asks 'where is this uni from'

**2. Daily-to-three-times-weekly delivery cadence**

Sushi operations need higher delivery frequency than restaurants more broadly. Suppliers who deliver only twice-weekly cannot serve premium sushi-ya at quality level. Confirm:

• Is daily delivery available year-round, or only during your peak?
• What is the minimum order quantity for daily delivery?
• What is the delivery time window (e.g., 09:00–10:30) and how consistent is it month-over-month?
• What is the protocol if a typhoon, public holiday, or supplier-side disruption affects scheduled delivery?

**3. Cold-chain documentation and inspection-friendly culture**

Suppliers who provide temperature logs and accept inspection-and-reject protocols build long-term trust. Suppliers who resist documentation are signaling they don't want their cold-chain examined.

• Will the delivery driver wait while you probe-check core temperatures?
• Will the supplier provide GPS+temperature transport telemetry on request?
• What is the written reject-and-replace policy if a shipment fails inspection?
• How fast can a replacement shipment arrive (same-day vs next-day) and at what cost?

**4. Volume-flexibility for sushi-ya weekly patterns**

Sushi-ya volume is lumpy in predictable ways — Friday/Saturday peaks 1.8–2.2x weekday baseline, lunch is generally 40–50% of dinner volume, special-occasion weeks (Mother's Day, Valentine's, year-end) can spike 2.5–3x normal. Suppliers need to handle this lumpiness without spot-pricing penalties or stockouts.

• Can you handle weekly volume variance of ±50% without spot pricing?
• What is the upside flexibility commitment for special-occasion weeks (need to know 2 weeks ahead)?
• What is the downside protection (minimum pay) you require for committed weekly allocation?

**Common pitfalls specific to HK sushi procurement:**

• **Pitfall 1 — Choosing on price during quiet season, then discovering during peak that supplier cannot scale.** Run a stress test by asking 'show me how you handled Mother's Day weekend volume last year' before committing.

• **Pitfall 2 — Accepting 'sushi-grade' as a meaningful designation.** It is not regulated. Always confirm the underlying freeze protocol or parasite-free certification.

• **Pitfall 3 — Ignoring substitution patterns until they impact a guest review.** Track substitution frequency in writing from week 1 of any new supplier relationship.

• **Pitfall 4 — Single-source procurement for premium grades.** Always maintain a secondary supplier with confirmed allocation, even at small volume, for AA Bafun and similar premium grades that have supply shocks.

• **Pitfall 5 — Not measuring delivery-temperature on receipt.** A probe thermometer costs HKD $300 and is the single most valuable procurement tool in a sushi-ya kitchen.
